# Differentiable or learned simulator experiment on one of your mechanisms

**Goal.** One of your built mechanisms (the inverted pendulum of `m45` or the arm of `m55`) modelled in NVIDIA Warp or Brax, with its parameters identified or a controller optimised by gradient descent through the simulation, and the result tested on the hardware against your hand-tuned baseline.

**Why.** Skills: differentiable simulation (gradients through contact-free dynamics, gradient checking, exploding gradients over long horizons), system identification, and sim-to-real. It closes the loop between Stage 1 simulation and Stage 2 hardware. Resources: `nvidia-warp`, `brax`, `pbdl-book`.

**Scope.** MVP, pick one. Identification: record trajectories on the hardware, fit masses, friction, damping and motor gain by minimising simulated-against-measured error, then redesign the controller on the identified model. Policy: optimise the gains of a linear or small neural controller by backpropagating a tracking or balance cost through the simulator. Both: gradients checked against finite differences, and the result run on hardware against the baseline. Stretch: both experiments, domain randomisation over the identified uncertainty.

**Architecture.** Logged hardware runs (inputs and measured states) are split into training and held-out sets. The differentiable simulator replays the same inputs from the same initial states; a loss compares the trajectories; the optimiser updates the parameters. The identified model feeds the controller design, and the new controller goes back to the hardware.

```
hardware logs ─▶ (u, x_meas) ─▶ diff. sim(θ) ─▶ x_sim ─▶ loss ─▶ ∇θ ─▶ optimiser ─▶ θ
                                                                                   │
                         hardware test ◀── controller redesign ◀───────────────────┘
```

**Stack.** Python, NVIDIA Warp (or JAX with Brax), NumPy, Matplotlib, the telemetry logs and firmware of the chosen mechanism. A CUDA GPU helps but Warp also runs on CPU. Nothing to buy.

**Milestones.** Mechanism modelled in the differentiable simulator (8 h) · gradient check against finite differences (4 h) · data collection on the hardware (5 h) · identification or policy optimisation by gradient descent (8 h) · hardware validation against the hand-tuned baseline (6 h) · write-up (4 h). Total 35 h.

**Done when.** Simulator gradients agree with central finite differences within 1 % for every parameter. Identification: on held-out runs, the identified model's RMS trajectory error is at least 30 % lower than the hand-measured parameters' error. Policy: the optimised controller beats the hand-tuned one in simulation on the chosen cost. In both cases the result runs on the hardware and is compared with the baseline on at least one stated metric (settling time, RMS angle, tracking error) over five runs each.

**Measure and report.** Identified parameters against hand-measured values with uncertainty; loss curves; trajectory error on training and held-out runs; hardware metric for baseline and new result, mean and spread over five runs; optimisation wall time; the horizon length at which gradients became unusable.

**Pitfalls.** Gradients that explode over long rollouts (shorten the horizon or use multiple shooting). Friction modelled with a sign function whose gradient is zero almost everywhere. Overfitting parameters to one recording. Unsynchronised logs, so a timing offset is absorbed into physical parameters. Claiming a sim gain that never reaches the hardware.

**References.** nvidia-warp, brax, pbdl-book, mit-6-832-underactuated, brunton-databook, m45, m55.

**Write-up.** Simulated-against-measured overlays before and after identification, the parameter table, the hardware comparison with error bars, and a paragraph on when differentiating through the simulator beat a black-box fit and when it did not.
