# Embedded Rust sensor node with telemetry to the year 3 backend

**Goal.** A `no_std` Rust sensor node on an ESP32 or STM32 that reads an environmental sensor through your own embedded-hal driver crate and reports over Wi-Fi (or a serial gateway) into the Kafka event pipeline and dashboard of project 28.

**Why.** Skills: `no_std` Rust, embedded-hal traits and driver design, async executors on a microcontroller (Embassy or RTIC), and MQTT or HTTP from constrained devices. It connects Stage 2 hardware to the Stage 1 backend in `m28`, and practises the ownership-driven driver style of `embedded-rust-book` and `rust-for-rustaceans`.

**Scope.** MVP: a driver crate for a BME280 (temperature, pressure, humidity) generic over `embedded_hal::i2c::I2c`, with host-side tests against a mocked bus; firmware on an ESP32-C3 using Embassy (or an STM32 with RTIC and a serial gateway); a reading every 10 s published over MQTT; a small bridge from the MQTT broker into a Kafka topic; a dashboard panel. Stretch: deep sleep between readings, an outbox in flash for offline buffering, OTA updates, publishing the driver crate to crates.io.

**Architecture.** A sensor task reads the BME280 and applies the datasheet compensation formulas. A network task keeps Wi-Fi and the MQTT session alive and drains a bounded channel of readings, each carrying a node id and a sequence number. On the backend, Mosquitto receives the messages and a bridge service produces them to Kafka, where the existing stream processor and dashboard of project 28 pick them up.

```
BME280 ─I2C─▶ sensor task ─▶ channel ─▶ net task ─Wi-Fi/MQTT─▶ Mosquitto ─▶ bridge ─▶ Kafka ─▶ stream job ─▶ dashboard
```

**Stack.** Rust stable, `embedded-hal` 1.0, `esp-hal` with Embassy (or `stm32` HAL crates with RTIC), `embedded-hal-mock` for tests, `probe-rs` or `espflash`, Mosquitto, a small Rust or Python MQTT-to-Kafka bridge, the dashboards from project 28 (Grafana or whatever you used there).

**Parts and budget.**
- ESP32-C3 or ESP32-S3 development board ($5–12), or an STM32 board with a Wi-Fi module ($15–25)
- BME280 environmental sensor breakout ($5–15)
- USB cable, breadboard and jumpers, reused from earlier projects ($0–5)
- Total: about $10–45

**Milestones.** Driver crate with host-side tests against a mocked I2C bus (8 h) · firmware with an async executor, the sensor task and Wi-Fi (9 h) · MQTT transport with reconnect and a bounded outbox (7 h) · MQTT-to-Kafka bridge and a dashboard panel (6 h) · 24-hour soak test and write-up (5 h). Total 35 h.

**Done when.** The driver has at least ten host tests and its compensated outputs match the worked examples in the Bosch datasheet to 0.01 °C, 1 Pa and 0.01 %RH. The firmware builds with `#![no_std]` and no allocator in the driver crate. Over a 24-hour soak, at least 99.5 % of sequence numbers arrive in Kafka and none are duplicated after de-duplication. The node reconnects within 30 s after the access point is switched off and on. Readings appear on the project 28 dashboard.

**Measure and report.** Delivery rate and gaps over 24 hours; end-to-end latency from sensor read to Kafka at p50 and p99; reconnect time after a Wi-Fi outage; firmware flash and RAM use; average current at 10 s intervals with and without light sleep; sensor reading against a reference thermometer.

**Pitfalls.** Self-heating: the BME280 reads 1–2 °C high when it is sampled continuously or mounted next to the radio. Blocking I2C calls inside an async task that starve the network stack. Losing readings during reconnect because the outbox is unbounded or absent. Clock-less devices stamping time: use the broker or bridge time, or SNTP. Kafka keys that scatter one node's readings across partitions and break ordering.

**References.** embedded-rust-book, rust-book, rust-for-rustaceans, making-embedded-systems, ddia, m28.

**Write-up.** A photo of the node, the dashboard over 24 hours, the delivery and latency table, the driver crate's API with one test, and a paragraph on what deep sleep would do to battery life.
